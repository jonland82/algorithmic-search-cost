two papers and a companion note on disorder and search

how much does order buy?

jonathan r. landers

an array can be nearly sorted without telling us where its disorder lies. the first paper asks what a bound on disorder alone can guarantee. a companion note checks how often that bound helps under uniform shuffling. the second paper asks what changes when comparisons can be reused across many searches.

the shared measure

\[V(A)=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(\operatorname{rank}_A(a_i)-i\bigr)^2\]

the papers and companion note

two papers / one note

01   /   one search

disorder potential and the cost of search

a limit \(V(A)\le E\) bounds how far a key can sit from its sorted position. a modified binary search ends with a short scan; a hidden minimum shows that the scan can be necessary.

tight worst-case cost

\[Q^*(n,E)=\Theta\!\left(\log n+\min\{n,\sqrt E\}\right)\]

families with the same potential budget can place disorder differently, so the budget alone does not describe every useful search feature.

companion note   /   after paper 01

potential across random permutations

an exact histogram for all \(9!\) orderings shows the shape of the same potential used in paper 01. exact moments and a tail bound then show how often a chosen disorder budget covers a uniform shuffle.

the typical uniform shuffle

\[\mathbb E V_n\sim n^3/12,\qquad E_{\mathrm{sat}}\sim n^2/2\]

most uniform shuffles eventually lie above the point where a scalar potential budget narrows the search window. this sets the context for paper 02's preprocessing tradeoff and for models that favor nearly sorted arrays.

02   /   repeated search

search under a disorder budget

for \(q\) searches on one fixed array, preprocessing becomes an investment. we can search each time, build an index from the displacement bound, or sort adaptively using the potential's inversion bound.

a reusable upper bound

\[T_q^*(n,E)=O\!\left(q\log n+\min\{qR_E,\;n\log(R_E+1),\;n[1+\log(1+E/n)]\}\right)\]

here \(R_E=1+D_E\), with \(D_E\) the displacement bound from \(E\). the potential also bounds inversions, making a cheaper index possible for nearly sorted arrays. learned hints help when comparisons verify their claims.

the through line

disorder limits what search must face. the permutation note shows that typical uniform shuffles are too disordered for the scalar budget to narrow the search window. location information and reusable preprocessing matter when the array has more favorable structure.
